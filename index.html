Mechanical engineering capstone project. This is our team of 10 in front of our final product, a drop testing machine built for Micromotion. During a 2 semester period our team went through all phases of the engineering process ideating, designing, testing, and ultimately building and painting this custom pneumatic drop testing machine.

I was one of 3 electromechanical engineers on the team. My specific contribution was PCB and power distribution to the machine. This is a whiteboard drawing outlining the overall electrical schematic for the machine.

Teagan and I spent months testing and designing the system. Here, we were excited to test the operation of our tower light, remote, pneumatic valve, LCD screen, and main PCB outside of the electrical box. On the left you can see version 2 of the main PCB. On the right is version 1.

This is the beginning of the integration of the electronics into the box. It became quite the challenge to find the best place for each component and plan out routing before setting off, but din rail and terminal blocks offered room for modularity.

We used machine shop resources and a hand drill to place all outside components on the box. The intent was to make safety features obvious and easy to access, while giving a clean and professional look.

This was one of the first full safety relay tests. Here we wanted to see that the safety relay was correctly activating the contactors, as well as verifying the tower light wiring responded correctly.

Adjacently, the steel frame of the machine was being TIG welded in the shop. TIG welding was determined the be the best method of joining the members since better heat control would prevent warpage on our tall frame where parallel beams were a nonnegotiable.

Frame fully welded and assembled standing up.

Finished frame being carried out for subassembly integration and painting.

The first assembly we tested on the frame had to be the carriage. The carriage is the most essential part of the machine and needs to ride up 4 linear rails perfectly. We used a manual hoist to test this component and ran into our first major assembly issue.. The frame was warped. Our most critical component began to bind on the linear rails.

After several meetings and much deliberation, we determined our frame had to work since the resources needed for a new one were to extensive. We began testing different amount of shim washers in the linear rails to achieve parallelism between the members.

Luckily, the shims worked, and our carriage could now ride up and down the rails within spec.

We proceeded with integration and were able to test the pneumatics and work out its kinks, as well as we painted the machine in Micromotion blue and safety yellow.

These were our first few tests with the full system assembled. We discovered an issue where a combination of cylinder pressure and damper stiffness allowed the drop leaf to spring back and kick the box, interfering with the drop. We worked to solve this by increasing the cylinder pressure and ordering several damper sizes to dial in the kick back.

Demonstration of a full drop sequence.

The flagship product I contributed to was an improved version of the widely used femoral nail. The purpose of this device is to aid the healing of a broken femur bone my being surgically installed into the center of the bone. Current models have a short coming that is adjustability. Do to their solid and unforgiving nature the nail itself has no ability to rotate or adjust for a persons gait. This new model solves that issue by introducing a two-piece design that can be locked in by a set screw. Much of my research went into contributing ideas, modeling new versions, and testing 3D printed, as well as machined versions of the nails.

At the end of my 2 years with the company and at the university the work on the femoral nail had reached its conclusion. After many years of development the product was ready for market and is now going through the process. I am assured that by the end of the year the device I helped develop will be installed in real patients helping them heal.

In one of our final tests we wanted to test the advantages of using 2 screws vs an additional 3rd screw below the nail seperation. Assembled here is the nail with a 3D printed grey sleeve simulating the femur bone. 2 bone screws are also installed.

The tests showed a massive improvement in torsion resistance, but this was at the cost of putting extra stress on the femur.

Several ideas were tested to improve the interface between the 2 sides of the nail and resist as much torsion as possible. The purpose is of course to prevent loosening of the device while installed in a user. The challenge was always maximizing locking force and friction. One promising idea was using an interference fit dogpoint set screw. This idea ended up falling short since the fit was quickly diminished and could not be reinstalled a reasonable amount of times. The final design seen in the second set of pictures uses a centric wave pattern to maximize the surface area between the 2 surfaces.

This is a prototype nail showing an old version of our nail slot. All prototypes were printed out of nylon 12 on a Formlabs Fuse 1 printer.

Another part of my work was manufacturing silicon suture holders. Suture holders are important in surgery where you need to use multiple sutures in the body. These little devices aid the surgeon in managing and organizing all the sutures.

At the end of the semester my team and I worked on an autonomous robot to compete in the class Mario Kart Balloon Battle competition. Our robot featured a pixycam for balloon identification, 2 ultrasonic distance sensors, ir proximity sensors, and a servo actuated Xacto blade to pop balloons with plans to add a limonene disk launcher.

The robot was controlled by a Raspberry Pi-Arduino interface with the pi handling the higher level controls and the Arduino managing the lower level quick respones.

2 weapons were allowed, a shooter and a lance. We devloped both weapons simultaneously. The lance was mounted on an extendable arm controlled by a servo.

We used omni wheels in our robots design to better navigate the course and allow our robot to strafe.

The shooeter we designed went through numerous iterations all to improve its assembly, loading, firing path, and adjustability. It shot 3D printed disks with a foam outer ring soaked in limonene, a corrosive oil that breaks down elastics and pops balloons on contact.

The firing mechanism used a powerful Merlin motor attached to a rubber lined wheel. Discs were loaded into a vertical holder and gravity fed into the mechanism. A pusher mechanism attached to a linkage arm was attached to a solenoid to push each disk into the flywheel.

The entire robot was powered by a lipo battery for adequate power. The shooter was supplied power by 2 relays. Unfortunately, during our testing of the shooter there was too many issues with friction that caused it to jam or not fire very far and meant it was too unreliable to use. With too little time to fix it we decided to shift resources and only use the lance.

During the course every team developed their own robot through a series of labs. At the end of the semester we designed our own challenge to further develop our robot platform in our own way. We decided to make a firefighting robot that could find fires and put them out!

The robot was able to find and navigate to fires by using 2 photoresistors to find the brightest light source direction and a thermistor to move towards a hear source. Once it was within range of the heat source it would activate its hat mounted fan to safely blow out the fire.

On the back of the robot we made sure to add LEDs and a buzzer. When the robot detects a fire and goes into pursuit mode it alternates its lights and plays a siren sound.

This project was an educational experience with Front Range Community College that took place over the course of an academic year. The goal of the project was to design an obstacle avoidance rover from the ground up with a team of 5 to compete at Great Sand Dunes and represent Front Range Community College among other schools in the state. We overcame many hurtles with this project. Our team of 5 developed into a team of 2 by the end of the first semester, but we carried on. After many long school nights, we successfully built a robot that was capable of completing all "Mars simulated courses" and even earned an award for its chassis structure. We later presented the rover at the COSGC Symposium, where we won the Top Robotics Poster Honors.

Our initial idea for steering DARWIN was to incorporate a zero degree turn system that essentially meant that the robot could make full rotations in place. As awesome as this idea seemed and as much as we wanted to incorporate it, upon implementation it introduced many issues and challenges. This video demonstrates one of our first tests of the system and a fatal flaw that we soon realized. As seen in the video the movement appears slow and weak. This is because the stepper motors we ordered did not have the required torque output to turn the wheels while the rover was on the ground. Unfortunately, we were not able to overcome this challenge with the time that we had and agreed to abandon the system.

This is another test of our steering system. Some improvements were made, but overall we could not stop the wheels from getting stuck. This was especially a key consideration when planning for harsher terrain such as sand or even rocks. It is also important to note in this video that we are purely testing the turning capabilities and not the obstacle avoidance feature as it was not integrated at the time of this testing.

This was our first successful test at the dunes. This was a simple course but demonstrates DARWIN's mobility as well as his calibration function. We found in our navigation testing that depending on the terrain DARWIN would experience vibrations that interfere with the Time of Flight(TOF) sensor motors. To fix this we implemented a calibration function that would occur every 10 seconds where the TOF sensor would look for the front two wheels and use that to recenter itself.

Course #2 at the dunes and here Adam explains DARWIN's calibration function.

Course #3 was our first good demonstration of DARWIN'S suspension system and why we chose to incorporate a rocker-bogie style suspension. Also mentioned here is our drift function. This was a crucial feature that allows us to navigate towards the finish line. In previous years teams had to incorporate a beacon system to navigate to the end of the course, but after many issues they decided to discontinue the practice. To solve this issue for our robot specifically, we implemented a dead reckoning system that would essentially have a "golden direction" that it would record when it is first turned on and drift functions that would give it a bias towards the golden direction when driving straight and not avoiding obstacles.

Course 4. Although our rocker-bogie suspension and 6-wheel drive helped us to maintain traction and overcome many obstacles, this was where we realized that our generic style wheels were less than ideal for the terrain. We would either get stuck from an awkward angle or encounter traction issues. Half of the tires were wrapped in electrical tape and after a few tries we were able to navigate to the end.

It is hard to tell in the video but course 5 was a steep incline on sand. We had many issues with sliding but DARWIN persevered.

This was one of the greatest achievements I have made in my engineering career. With numerous challenges and hours spent on the project it was surreal seeing it all come together in the end. I am so proud of my partner and I in what we have achieved.

Also in this picture is our carrying handle that we made to easily transport DARWIN. We didn't have any great spots to pick up or hold the robot so this was a great solution.

This was our poster presentation setup at the engineering symposium at CU Boulder.

Also pictured here is our before and after models of our shape optimized parts. Using this technology in fusion we were able to make organic looking parts that used up to 60% less material while maintaining structural strength.

Our final poster for the symposium where we won best robotics poster.

My first attempt at hydrodipping a large item like a front splitter in carbon fiber to be used on the underside of a Volkswagen Beetle. This project required extensive prep work to smooth out the previously textured surface.

The splitter is made up of 3 parts. Here I have the 3 parts sanded and primed ready to be dipped.

These are a series of photos after the splitter had been clear coated. The final paint job had a nice shine and a clean look.

Unfortunately, the finished splitter underwent some critical damage and needed to be repaired. All broken parts were reattached and meticulously reworked to a factory look with extensive bondo and sanding work along with all imperfections and impact damage. The part was then redipped in a carbon fiber film to achieve the desired look.

These are all of the broken pieces of the splitter sanded and primed to get a paintable surface again.

The splitter was broken in several places. It was hit hard by a large water puddle and managed to break the ends and a small piece that attached the middle to the right side.

The parts required extensive amounts of labor to restore, but I managed to get them back together using 2 brackets and some screws that I would cut with a dremel and sand down flat. Damaged parts were also reinforced and reattached with epoxy. Once I got the splitter back together it still had numerous damaged areas from impacts that I went over with Bondo and sand paper to ultimately achieve this factory look again.

One more layer of adhesion promoting primer was applied to hopefully ensure the final product was long lasting.

At the end of the project I was able to restore the splitter and I managed to achieve an even better finish this time. To further reinforce the product, garage floor clear coat was used on the splitter to protect the paint from UV and any impacts.
